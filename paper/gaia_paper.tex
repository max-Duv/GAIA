\title{Project GAIA: GPU Power Attack Characterization, Detection, and Mitigation on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Max Duverneuil}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Computer Science and Engineering\\
Penn State University\\
mpd5945@psu.edu}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Modern AI data centers rely on dense GPU deployments whose power profiles
create new attack surfaces for co-located adversaries.  We present
Project GAIA, a three-thrust investigation of GPU microarchitectural
power attacks on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor (Blackwell, sm\_110).
In Thrust~1, we design and characterize six adversarial workload
conditions---sustained cache-eviction stress, compute-only saturation,
10\,kHz pulsed attacks, a temperature-synchronized variant, and their
baselines---across 10 randomized trials.  Attacks elevate module-rail
power from 45\,W (idle) to 89\,W (sustained stress) while,
paradoxically, \emph{reducing} co-located victim latency from
1.45\,ms to 1.20\,ms due to GPU clock boosting.
In Thrust~2, we evaluate two detection mechanisms:
a power-level threshold detector (100\% precision, 72.4\% recall,
0\% false-positive rate, 4.3\,s mean detection latency) and a
logistic-regression classifier over windowed telemetry features
(88.0\% accuracy, 84.2\% F1 via leave-one-trial-out cross-validation),
supplemented by Welch PSD spectral characterization and GPU
performance-counter analysis.
In Thrust~3, we close the loop with two mitigation strategies:
devfreq-based clock capping (18.4\% power reduction, 3.3$\times$
latency cost) and graceful adversarial-kernel termination, integrated
with a Prometheus-compatible telemetry exporter for industry-standard
monitoring.  All pairwise comparisons are statistically significant
($p < 0.001$, Cohen's $d > 1.6$).  Our results demonstrate that
user-level GPU adversarial kernels measurably manipulate server power,
that low-overhead telemetry can detect them with zero false positives,
and that dynamic clock management and process termination provide
effective real-time countermeasures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Hyper-specialized GPU data centers have become the de facto cornerstone
of the modern AI revolution.  The Northern Virginia region alone hosts
the world's highest concentration of data centers~\cite{eia2026}, and
utilities such as Dominion Energy are preparing for $3\times$ their
current peak load to accommodate future GPU-centric
infrastructure~\cite{dominion2026}.  This concentration creates a
critical vulnerability: \emph{coordinated power attacks on AI data
centers may propagate across the power grid under adversarial
conditions}~\cite{choukse2025}.  Recent collaborative work by
Microsoft, OpenAI, and NVIDIA has demonstrated that harmonized power
swings from large training workloads can physically damage grid
infrastructure~\cite{watchus2025,semieng2025}.

Prior work has established the effectiveness of power attacks against
traditional multi-tenant data centers (MTDCs).  Islam et
al.~\cite{islam2017} demonstrated that co-located tenants can induce
measurable power fluctuations, and subsequent work showed voltage side
channels exploitable for timing attacks~\cite{islam2018}.  Xu et
al.~\cite{xu2014} characterized power attacks as an increasing threat
to data center availability.  Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025}
recently demonstrated that GPU cache-eviction priority hints have
significant security and performance implications.  However, modern
GPU-based AI training facilities have distinct power, memory, and
computational load profiles compared to traditional compute servers;
therefore, new attack models are likely to differ.

This raises a fundamental research question: \emph{How can power
attacks on GPU hardware in modern AI data centers be designed,
evaluated, detected, and mitigated to prevent grid disruptions?}

We address this question with Project GAIA, structured around three
research thrusts:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Thrust~1 (T1):} Design benign-looking microarchitectural
  workloads that force co-located training applications to draw more
  power, driving multi-tenant GPU servers toward oversubscription and
  system instability.
\item \textbf{Thrust~2 (T2):} Develop and evaluate defense mechanisms
  for detecting power attacks using GPU hardware telemetry and power
  services.
\item \textbf{Thrust~3 (T3):} Prototype an end-to-end closed-loop
  monitoring pipeline to flag anomalous power-driven load and apply
  real-time mitigation.
\end{itemize}

Our experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor developer
kit (Blackwell architecture, sm\_110, 130\,W TDP) using hardware
power sensors (INA3221 via sysfs) and GPU telemetry (devfreq, thermal
zones).  All six conditions are randomized across 10 trials with
15-second phases, providing statistical power for paired comparisons.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:threat} describes the threat model.
Section~\ref{sec:method} details the experimental methodology.
Sections~\ref{sec:t1}--\ref{sec:t3} present results for each thrust.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses findings and limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}
% ===========================================================================

We assume a malicious or compromised tenant with user-level access
co-located on a multi-tenant GPU training server.  The attacker's goal
is to manipulate the temporal power profile of the shared server where
other benign AI training jobs are running.  The attacker has no root,
firmware, physical, or facility-level power-system access.  The
attacker uses two classes of controlled adversarial kernels:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cache-eviction kernels:} CUDA kernels using PTX
  \texttt{st.global.wt} (write-through) and \texttt{ld.global.cg}
  (cache-global) instructions to repeatedly thrash the L2 cache,
  forcing high memory-bus utilization and power draw.
\item \textbf{Compute-saturation kernels:} INT32 multiply-add loops
  designed to saturate arithmetic pipelines without significant memory
  traffic, isolating the compute contribution to power.
\end{enumerate}

Additionally, we consider \emph{pulsed} variants that modulate attack
intensity at 10\,kHz with 50\% duty cycle, emulating attempts to
induce subsynchronous resonance at targeted frequencies.  Experiments
are conducted in a safe, isolated research testbed with hardware power
monitoring.  We use power variance, voltage sag, throttling events,
and workload slowdown as measurable proxies for facility-level
instability~\cite{islam2017,yandrofski2022}.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Platform}

All experiments run on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor developer kit:
Blackwell GPU (sm\_110), 130\,W TDP, CUDA~13.0, driver~580.00.
The platform provides hardware-level power measurement via six
on-board INA3221 current/voltage sensors accessible through sysfs,
eliminating the need for external power meters.  The module rail
sensor reports voltage ($\sim$19.75\,V) and current, from which
instantaneous power is derived.  GPU clock frequency is read from the
devfreq interface (\texttt{/sys/class/devfreq/gpu-gpc-0/cur\_freq})
and GPU temperature from the thermal zone
(\texttt{/sys/class/thermal/thermal\_zone1/temp}).  Telemetry is
sampled at the maximum stable rate supported by sysfs polling,
achieving 3.5--4.0\,Hz.

\subsection{Thrust~1: Attack Characterization}

We evaluate six experimental conditions in a randomized-order design
across 10 independent trials, each with 15-second phases:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Victim alone} (baseline): A matrix-multiply victim
  kernel runs without any co-located adversary.
\item \textbf{Sustained stress} (DC): Cache-eviction + compute
  adversarial kernel runs continuously at 100\% duty cycle alongside
  the victim.
\item \textbf{Cache-only stress}: Only cache-eviction primitives
  active (no compute saturation).
\item \textbf{Compute-only stress}: Only INT32 compute saturation
  active (no cache thrashing).
\item \textbf{Pulsed stress} (10\,kHz, 50\% duty): Full
  cache+compute kernel modulated at 10\,kHz using CUDA
  \texttt{clock64()} busy-wait timing.
\item \textbf{Temperature-synchronized stress}: 10\,kHz pulsed
  attack launched only after detecting a $>$0.5\,$^\circ$C GPU
  temperature rise (via thermal zone sysfs, 15\,s timeout),
  demonstrating that user-level thermal side channels can
  synchronize attack timing to victim computation.  Each trial
  records whether the trigger was thermal or timeout.
\end{enumerate}

A mechanism-isolation sweep additionally varies pulsing frequency
across nine values (0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, and
10{,}000\,Hz) at fixed 50\% duty cycle, and separately varies
duty cycle (25\%, 50\%, 75\%) at fixed 10\,kHz, across 5 trials
per setting.

In addition to INA3221 power and devfreq clock telemetry, we record
GPU and memory utilization percentages via NVML
(\texttt{nvmlDeviceGetUtilizationRates}), providing performance-counter
data for multi-modal detection analysis.

Phase order is randomized per trial, and a warmup trial is discarded.

\subsection{Thrust~2: Detection}

We evaluate two complementary detection approaches:

\textbf{Power-level threshold detector.}
A sliding-window detector computes mean power over the most recent
telemetry buffer.  An anomaly is flagged when mean power exceeds a
configurable threshold $\theta = 60$\,W, chosen to separate the
$\sim$45\,W idle baseline from the $\sim$84\,W attack power level.
The detector operates on raw INA3221 sensor readings with Kalman
filtering for noise smoothing.

\textbf{Logistic regression classifier.}
We extract four features per non-overlapping window of 50 telemetry
samples: mean power, power standard deviation, minimum voltage, and
voltage range.  Windows are constructed \emph{within} each
(trial, phase) pair to avoid cross-boundary contamination.  A
logistic regression classifier is evaluated via leave-one-trial-out
cross-validation, with a \texttt{StandardScaler} fit exclusively on
the training fold of each split.

We also apply PCA to the standardized feature matrix to assess
state separability between benign and attack workloads.

\subsection{Thrust~3: Closed-Loop Mitigation}

The mitigation pipeline integrates detection and two response
strategies:

\textbf{Strategy~A: Clock capping.}
(1)~The power-level detector monitors real-time telemetry;
(2)~upon detecting an anomaly ($\bar{P} > 60$\,W), the system caps
the GPU clock via the Jetson devfreq interface
(\texttt{max\_freq}: $1575 \to 630$\,MHz);
(3)~after the attack phase ends, the clock is restored.

\textbf{Strategy~B: Graceful termination.}
Upon anomaly detection, the system identifies and terminates the
adversarial kernel's process (SIGTERM, with SIGKILL fallback after
2\,s timeout), eliminating the attack source entirely.  Exit
confirmation, kill method (SIGTERM vs SIGKILL), and elapsed kill
time are recorded.  Post-kill power and victim latency recovery
are measured for the remainder of the phase.

Each trial runs five phases in sequence: \emph{attack\_unmitigated}
(attack with no defense), \emph{attack\_mitigated} (attack with
active detection and clock capping), \emph{attack\_terminated}
(attack with detection-triggered process kill),
\emph{victim\_clamped\_baseline}
(no attack but clock still capped, measuring clamping overhead), and
\emph{recovery\_baseline} (no attack, full clock restored).  Five
independent trials provide paired statistical comparisons.

\textbf{Prometheus telemetry exporter.}
To bridge the gap between research instrumentation and
industry-standard monitoring, we implement a lightweight HTTP server
that exports all GPU telemetry metrics (power, voltage, clock,
temperature, utilization, anomaly flags) in Prometheus exposition
format on port~9400, compatible with standard Prometheus/Grafana monitoring stacks
deployed in production data centers.  A pre-built Grafana dashboard
(\texttt{grafana\_dashboard.json}) provides seven panels covering
power, temperature, clock, voltage, utilization, and anomaly status.

\subsection{The Clock-Boosting Latency Paradox}

A counterintuitive finding is that the victim kernel runs
\emph{faster} under attack: 1.20\,ms (all attack conditions)
versus 1.45\,ms when alone.  GPU telemetry reveals the mechanism:
under load from the co-located adversary, the GPU's dynamic
voltage--frequency scaling (DVFS) boosts the clock from a variable
$1310 \pm 216$\,MHz (victim alone, with idle periods triggering
downclocking) to a sustained 1575\,MHz (all attack conditions).
The victim benefits from this higher clock despite sharing the GPU.

This has an important security implication: \emph{latency degradation
alone is not a reliable indicator of a co-located power attack on
modern GPUs with aggressive DVFS.}  The attack's impact is on power
draw and thermal state, not necessarily on execution time.
Temperature rises from 44.3\,$^\circ$C (alone) to 47.7\,$^\circ$C
(sustained stress).

\subsection{Mechanism Isolation}

The frequency sweep (0.5\,Hz to 10\,kHz at 50\% duty, 5 trials
per setting) reveals a DVFS interaction effect: at sub-Hz pulsing,
the GPU's dynamic voltage--frequency scaling has time to downclock
during idle phases, reducing time-averaged power.  At higher
frequencies ($>$50\,Hz), the idle phase is too short for DVFS to
react, and power converges toward the DC value.  This shows that
the power elevation is primarily from aggregate compute load
modulated by DVFS response time, not resonance at specific
frequencies.

The duty-cycle sweep (25\%, 50\%, 75\% at fixed 10\,kHz) directly
measures the relationship between active fraction and power draw,
confirming that power scales approximately linearly with duty cycle.
This validates that the attacker can control the magnitude of
power injection by tuning the active/idle ratio.

\subsection{PCA State Separability}

Principal component analysis on the four-feature windows (mean power,
power std, voltage min, voltage range) shows strong separability
between benign and attack workloads.  The first two principal
components capture 84.5\% of the total variance (PC1: 62.7\%, PC2:
21.8\%), indicating that the power-telemetry feature space
provides a low-dimensional manifold on which attack and benign states
are well separated.

\subsection{Logistic Regression Classifier}

The logistic regression classifier, evaluated via leave-one-trial-out
cross-validation with per-fold standardization, achieves:
accuracy 88.0\%, precision 88.9\%, recall 80.0\%, F1-score 84.2\%
across 50 windows (30 benign, 20 attack) drawn from 10 held-out
trials.  Windows are constructed within each (trial, phase) pair
to prevent information leakage across trial or phase boundaries.

\subsection{Spectral Characterization}

We apply Welch PSD estimation to the power time series within each
phase to characterize the low-frequency power envelope.  At the
$\sim$4\,Hz sysfs sampling rate ($\sim$2\,Hz Nyquist), the 10\,kHz
attack pulsing frequency is fundamentally unresolvable; however, the
spectral analysis reveals that attack phases exhibit significantly
higher total spectral power and distinct peak frequencies compared to
baseline.  This confirms that even at low sampling rates, the power
envelope carries discriminative information---the detection mechanism
exploits the time-averaged power elevation rather than the
high-frequency modulation pattern.

\subsection{GPU Performance Counters}

NVML utilization rates (\texttt{nvmlDeviceGetUtilizationRates})
provide an additional telemetry dimension.  GPU compute utilization
rises from near-zero (victim alone, with inter-kernel idle gaps) to
sustained high utilization under all attack conditions, while memory
utilization differentiates cache-heavy attacks (high memory
utilization) from compute-only attacks (low memory utilization).
These counters complement power telemetry and could improve classifier
accuracy in future multi-modal detection approaches.

\subsection{Sampling Rate Limitations}

The $\sim$4\,Hz sysfs telemetry sampling rate fundamentally limits
frequency-domain analysis.  The 10\,kHz attack pulsing is far above
the $\sim$2\,Hz Nyquist limit, making FFT-based spectral analysis
ineffective for resolving attack modulation patterns.  Our detection
therefore relies on power-level anomaly, which remains effective
because the attack's time-averaged power draw is distinguishable
from baseline regardless of modulation frequency.  On data center
GPUs with DCGM telemetry (10--100\,Hz), the spectral approach
originally proposed would become viable.

\subsection{Latency--Power Tradeoff}

The mitigation introduces a significant latency penalty.  Victim
latency increases from 1.20\,ms (unmitigated attack) to 3.94\,ms
(mitigated attack), a $3.3\times$ increase.  However, a portion of
this latency increase is inherent to the clock cap itself:
\emph{victim\_clamped\_baseline} (no attack, clock capped) shows
2.97\,ms latency---a 1.51\,ms overhead versus the 1.46\,ms recovery
baseline.  The decomposition is:

\begin{align}
\text{Gross latency change} &= B - A = 3.94 - 1.20 = +2.75\,\text{ms} \\
\text{Clamping overhead} &= C - D = 2.97 - 1.46 = +1.51\,\text{ms} \\
\text{Net attack effect} &= (B-A) - (C-D) = +1.24\,\text{ms}
\end{align}

The paired $t$-test between unmitigated and mitigated attack latency
is significant ($t = -563.0$, $p < 0.001$), confirming that the
power reduction is achieved at a measurable performance cost.

\subsection{Detection-to-Mitigation Latency}

The closed-loop system detects the anomaly within 4.2--4.3\,s of
attack onset, then applies the clock cap within the same telemetry
polling cycle.  All five trials triggered mitigation exactly once
per attack phase, with no false triggers during benign phases.
After mitigation, module-rail voltage recovers from 19.736\,V
(attack) to 19.744\,V (mitigated), consistent with reduced current
draw.

\subsection{Graceful Kernel Termination}

The termination strategy (Strategy~B) provides a more decisive
response: upon detecting the power anomaly, the system kills the
adversarial process, eliminating the attack entirely.  Unlike clock
capping, which constrains both attacker and victim equally,
termination selectively removes the adversarial workload while
allowing the victim to resume at full clock speed.  The
\emph{attack\_terminated} phase demonstrates that power returns to
baseline levels within one telemetry cycle after kill, with victim
latency recovering to pre-attack values.  This approach is more
suitable when the adversarial process can be identified and isolated.

\subsection{Prometheus Integration}

The Prometheus-compatible telemetry exporter serves all eight
monitored metrics (power, voltage, clock, temperature, GPU
utilization, memory utilization, anomaly flag, mean detector power)
on a standard HTTP endpoint with Prometheus exposition format.
Smoke testing confirms correct metric formatting and real-time
updates at 1\,s polling intervals.  This enables integration with
production Prometheus/Grafana monitoring stacks, bridging the gap
between research instrumentation and industry-standard observability
as required for data center deployment.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Key Findings}

Our results address the three questions posed by the GAIA proposal:

\textbf{Can user-level GPU workloads measurably manipulate server
power?}  Yes.  All five attack conditions elevate module-rail power
significantly above baseline, with sustained stress reaching
$1.95\times$ the idle power level.  Peak power (103\,W) reaches
79\% of TDP.  The temperature-synchronized variant demonstrates that
an attacker can use GPU thermal readings as a user-level side channel
to time attack launch to victim computation, without requiring any
privileged access.  The voltage drop, while small on the module rail
(8\,mV), would compound across hundreds of GPUs in a data center
rack.

\textbf{Can low-overhead telemetry detect these attacks?}  Yes, with
caveats.  The power-level threshold detector achieves zero false
positives and 100\% precision, making it suitable for deployment
where false alarms are costly.  The 72.4\% recall and 4.3\,s
detection latency are acceptable for a first-response system, though
insufficient for latency-critical applications.  The logistic
regression classifier (88\% accuracy) demonstrates that simple
ML models over power telemetry features can distinguish attack from
benign workloads with moderate accuracy.

\textbf{Can mitigation be applied in real time?}  Yes, via two
complementary strategies.  Devfreq clock capping reduces power by
18.4\% with a 3.3$\times$ latency cost, representing the
fundamental tradeoff between power security and compute performance.
Graceful kernel termination provides a more targeted response by
eliminating the adversarial process, allowing the victim to resume at
full performance.  The Prometheus telemetry exporter enables
integration with industry-standard monitoring stacks for production
deployment.

\subsection{The Clock-Boosting Paradox}

The finding that victim latency \emph{decreases} under attack is
significant for GPU security monitoring.  Traditional
intrusion-detection systems that monitor for performance degradation
would \emph{miss} this class of attack entirely.  The attack's
primary impact---elevated power draw---is invisible to the victim
workload, which sees only the benefit of a boosted clock.  This
motivates the use of hardware power telemetry (not performance
counters alone) for attack detection, consistent with the approach
taken by Yandrofski et al.~\cite{yandrofski2022} for real-time
systems.

\subsection{Platform Considerations}

The Jetson AGX Thor is a development platform, not a data center GPU
server.  Several differences affect generalizability:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Integrated GPU:} The Jetson's iGPU shares the SoC
  power domain with the CPU, unlike discrete data center GPUs (A100,
  H100) with independent power planes.  Discrete GPUs support
  \texttt{nvidia-smi --power-limit}, which the Jetson does not;
  our devfreq clock-capping mitigation is Jetson-specific.
\item \textbf{Telemetry rate:} The 3.5--4\,Hz sysfs sampling rate is
  far below what DCGM provides on data center GPUs ($\sim$10--100\,Hz).
  Higher sampling rates would enable spectral analysis of attack
  modulation patterns as originally proposed.
\item \textbf{Scale:} Single-GPU experiments cannot capture
  multi-GPU resonance effects or rack-level power aggregation.
\end{itemize}

Despite these limitations, the Jetson provides ground-truth hardware
power measurement through on-board INA3221 sensors---a capability
that is typically unavailable on discrete GPU systems without external
power meters.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

\textbf{Benign high-power workloads.}  The power-level detector's
60\,W threshold may produce false positives under legitimate
heavy-compute workloads (e.g., large model training).  Testing
against benign high-power workloads is needed to validate the
detector's discrimination ability.

\textbf{Multi-tenant isolation.}  Our experiments co-locate victim
and attacker on the same GPU without MPS or MIG partitioning.
Evaluating attacks under hardware isolation mechanisms (NVIDIA MIG
on A100/H100) would determine whether memory-space partitioning
mitigates the power-channel attack.

\textbf{Adaptive attackers.}  A power-aware attacker could modulate
intensity to stay below the detection threshold.  Adaptive detection
strategies (e.g., anomaly detection over longer windows or
rate-of-change metrics) would be needed.

\textbf{Scale-up.}  Extending to multi-GPU and rack-level experiments
would enable measurement of aggregate power effects and grid-coupling
dynamics as described by Choukse et al.~\cite{choukse2025}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Data center power attacks.}
Islam et al.~\cite{islam2017} provided the first systematic look at
power attacks in multi-tenant data centers, demonstrating that
co-located VMs can manipulate aggregate server power draw.  Their
subsequent work~\cite{islam2018} exploited Ohm's law to construct
voltage side channels for timing attacks.  Xu et al.~\cite{xu2014}
surveyed power attacks as threats to data center availability.

\textbf{GPU microarchitectural attacks.}
Yandrofski et al.~\cite{yandrofski2022} demonstrated that adversarial
CUDA kernels can create ``powerful enemies'' on NVIDIA GPUs,
manipulating power draw and disrupting real-time system guarantees.
Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025} showed that GPU cache-eviction priority
hints (as we exploit in our cache-eviction kernels) have significant
security implications.  Nazari et al.~\cite{nazari2023} demonstrated
adversarial attacks against ML-based resource provisioning systems.

\textbf{AI data center power management.}
Choukse et al.~\cite{choukse2025} characterized power stabilization
challenges for AI training datacenters in a Microsoft/OpenAI/NVIDIA

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Project GAIA demonstrates that GPU microarchitectural power attacks
are a viable threat on modern AI hardware.  User-level adversarial
kernels---including temperature-synchronized variants---elevate GPU
power by up to $1.95\times$ baseline without triggering hardware
throttling, and paradoxically improve victim performance through
clock boosting.  A power-level threshold detector achieves zero
false positives with 72.4\% recall, supplemented by spectral
characterization and performance-counter analysis.  Two closed-loop
mitigation strategies---devfreq clock capping (18.4\% power
reduction, $3.3\times$ latency cost) and graceful adversarial-kernel
termination---demonstrate complementary tradeoffs for real-time
response, integrated with a Prometheus-compatible telemetry exporter
for industry-standard monitoring.

These experimental results establish the viability of a complete
attack--detect--mitigate--monitor pipeline on a Jetson platform.
Future work will extend the evaluation to multi-GPU data center
hardware with NVIDIA DCGM telemetry, higher sampling rates enabling
spectral analysis, MIG isolation, and adaptive adversarial
strategies.
